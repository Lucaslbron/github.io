1. Requirements & Design Constraints
The project began with fixed locations for the wheels, handlebars/control linkage, and motor. The chassis therefore had
to be designed around existing geometric constraints while preserving operator space and room for future components.
The presentation also identifies the chassis material as Aluminum 6061-T6, with 2.5 in OD × 0.25 in wall round tube for
the main chassis and 2.0 in OD × 0.25 in wall tube for the later rear supports. ■
Design constraints carried through the project:
 Fixed wheel and control-device locations
 Motor mounting/interface requirements
 Operator packaging and foot clearance
 Space for future components and seat hardware
 Structural strength and acceptable deflection
 Manufacturable geometry and practical material selection
 The original presentation notes that the rear wheel clearance was relatively tight and identifies this as an area
that would need more tolerance in a real production design.
2. Chassis Design & Structural Iteration
The first major design iteration was evaluated without the rear vertical supports. The analysis showed that the initial
chassis was substantially under-supported, so two rear supports were introduced and the model was re-evaluated. This
is the clearest example in the project of the design-analysis-redesign loop that should be emphasized in the portfolio.
Structural results: maximum stress decreased from 5.233 × 104 psi to 2.786 × 103 psi after the support
modification. Maximum deflection decreased from 5.512 in to 0.03444 in. Minimum factor of safety increased
from 0.76 to 14.
The presentation attributes the loading to a 200 lb operator and a 400 lb fan assembly. The final design therefore
demonstrates how simulation was used to identify a major structural weakness and guide a specific geometric
modification rather than simply documenting a final FEA result.


=== PAGE 3 ===
3. Seat Frame Design & Nonlinear Loading
The seat was developed around operator packaging, seating dimensions, clearance from the chassis, and room for
cushioning. The modeled seat uses Aluminum 7075-T6 with a 0.2294 in sheet thickness. The initial dimensions were
established from an estimated 12 in × 12 in seating area and a 16 in overall height.
The analysis modeled a changing load over a two-second interval. The presentation describes the main seat load
decreasing from 200 lb to 150 lb while 50 lb was transferred to the backrest. The initial study produced a factor of safety
of 1.862 and identified the need for additional support/suspension to mitigate displacement.
 This section is worth keeping in the deep dive because it demonstrates that the seat was not treated as an
isolated CAD component: its geometry, operator interface, loading, and later suspension system were
developed as connected design problems.
4. Seat Suspension: Iteration & Fatigue Study
The suspension design was integrated into the existing seat/chassis geometry and evaluated under the defined loading
and fixture conditions. The second iteration modified the connection geometry after SolidWorks limitations prevented the
intended curved connection from being modeled as planned.
 Iteration results: maximum stress decreased from 3.013 × 104 psi to 1.788 × 104 psi; maximum
displacement decreased from 0.66 in to 0.40 in; and minimum factor of safety increased from 2.432 to 4.097.
An important limitation should remain visible in the portfolio: the presentation reports that the damage and cycle-life plots
contained an unresolved error, so fatigue life could not be quantified reliably. The second design nevertheless showed
improved stress, displacement, and minimum FOS.

5. Battery Pack & Thermal Study
The battery pack was designed as the vehicle's power source and evaluated with a preliminary thermal model. The study
used a convection coefficient of 10 W/m·K and included rudimentary fins as a temporary cooling approach.
 The simulated maximum temperature reached 770,100 °F. The presentation explicitly recognizes this as
nonphysical for the intended battery system and concludes that the battery would experience catastrophic
thermal runaway long before this temperature. The result is therefore best presented as a modeling
limitation / boundary-condition problem, not as a physical prediction.
For the portfolio, I would keep this result because it demonstrates engineering judgment: the important outcome is not
the extreme number itself, but recognizing that the simulation setup does not represent a physically valid operating
condition and identifying the need for a new thermal solution.
6. Fixed-Wing Aerodynamics & Structural Analysis
A fixed wing was designed from scratch for the airbike and evaluated using flow simulation. The wing was modeled
without onboard flight-control surfaces. The presentation reports that the original submission used 55 mph while the
updated study shown in the final presentation used 75 mph.
 Reported results: maximum stress of approximately 4,795 psi, maximum displacement of approximately
0.6859 in, and minimum factor of safety of approximately 0.605 for the modeled balsa configuration.
The low FOS led to a material/design change: the wing material was changed to Aluminum 6061-T6 in the larger vehicle
model. This also exposed a major vehicle-level optimization problem because the modeled wings were still solid rather
than internally ribbed/sparred.


=== PAGE 5 ===
7. Propeller / Fan Flow Study
The propeller/fan system was designed to propel the airbike and included both the blade airfoils and protective cage. A
rotating-region flow study was used to examine pressure, force, trajectories, and particle behavior.
 The study reported an average normal force of approximately 798 lbf. The presentation then compared this
with an overall vehicle mass of approximately 3,000 lb and concluded that the modeled thrust would not be
sufficient to move the vehicle as configured.
Rather than treating that as a final failure, the presentation identifies several optimization paths: reducing wing mass,
increasing fan size, optimizing blade count/geometry, and selecting lighter materials while maintaining a suitable factor of
safety. This is a strong vehicle-level engineering discussion and should remain in the deep dive.
8. Battery-Pack Heat Exchanger
The heat exchanger was developed to manage the battery pack's thermal profile. The flow study modeled R-134a and oil
and used mass-flow boundary conditions. The presentation calculated a refrigerant mass flow of 1.17 lb/s and an oil
mass flow of 0.35 lb/s for the modeled conditions.
The study produced an R-134a outlet temperature of -9.81 °F. The presentation investigates this result and attributes the
localized cooling to pressure-drop/expansion effects associated with the unpressurized outlet boundary condition. It then
identifies a more realistic pressurized outlet condition of approximately 50–60 psia as a way to remove that modeling
artifact.

9. Final Design & Engineering Assessment
The final portfolio story should not claim that every subsystem is production-ready. The presentation itself identifies
several areas requiring further engineering: additional rear-wheel clearance, a more cost-effective structural material
selection, improved wing structural architecture and weight reduction, a new battery cooling approach, and further
fan/vehicle optimization.
 The stronger story is that the project progressed from a set of fixed geometric constraints into an integrated
airbike concept, while simulation repeatedly exposed design weaknesses and informed subsequent changes.

10. My Contribution
Primary responsibilities: chassis CAD and structural iteration; seat and suspension design; integration of vehicle
subsystems; setup and interpretation of multiple SolidWorks simulation studies; wing and fan modeling; battery and
heat-exchanger studies.
Engineering judgment demonstrated: identifying excessive chassis deflection and adding supports; recognizing the
limitations of the fatigue and thermal models; identifying the low wing FOS; recognizing that vehicle mass made the
modeled fan force insufficient; and proposing specific redesign directions.
11. Engineering Takeaways
 Simulation should drive design decisions: the chassis support redesign is the clearest example of analysis directly
changing geometry.
 Subsystem results must be considered at vehicle level: the fan study became meaningful only when compared
against the approximately 3,000 lb vehicle mass.
 Model limitations matter: the thermal and fatigue studies contain explicitly acknowledged limitations, which should be
presented rather than hidden.
 Weight and manufacturability remain future optimization targets: the final concept provides a basis for further
structural and aerodynamic refinement.
